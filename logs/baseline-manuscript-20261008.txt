Finite-ensemble observability of the mean-field
convergence rate in consensus-based optimization
Rahul Kumar Mishra
* Independent Researcher, Ranchi, Jharkhand, India.

Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): mishra.rahul98@gmail.com;
Abstract
Consensus-based optimization (CBO) has a convergence guarantee for the implementable algorithm, assembled from a convergence result in mean-field law, a
quantitative mean-field approximation and a discretization estimate. The guarantee is asymptotic in the ensemble size, and the constants carrying the parameter
dependence sit in the prefactor rather than in the exponent. This paper asks a
narrower question: which of those parameter dependencies are visible as effects
on the slope fitted to a finite run, under experimental protocols that are stated
and audited rather than assumed. Across 31 320 coupled realizations distributed
over 2 346 cells of 12 to 20 realizations each, we report four results.
First, an inverse-temperature rule matched by objective spread produces an
apparent dimension-dependent degradation of the fitted exponent of 0.96 from
d = 2 to d = 40. Calibrating the reference ensemble so that τ0,∞ =
ESS0 (J∞ )/J∞ equals a target τ0 , and fitting only over ensemble sizes J ≥
1/τ0 at which that target is attainable, reduces it to 0.054, 0.059 and 0.078
at τ0 = 0.5, 0.1 and 0.01. Both restrictions matter: a probe-calibrated fit
over the full grid gives 0.030, 0.099 and 0.553, and the formal dimensionconcentration interaction that is resolved over the full grid, +0.0305 with interval
[+0.0123, +0.0479], is not resolved over the restricted one, +0.0073 with
[−0.0091, +0.0227].
Second, the remaining contrast is confounded and we report it as such. The
nested subensembles whose error is measured are systematically further from
their concentration target at d = 40 than at d = 2 at every size measured.
A dedicated experiment that controls finite-ensemble concentration exactly, at
the cost of one inverse temperature per ensemble size and therefore of changing
the algorithm along the fitted J -curve, does not make the contrast vanish but is
inconclusive about the mechanism of the residual.
Third, outside the parameter condition 2λ > dσ 2 the onset is not resolvable from
the boundary r = dσ 2 /(2λ) = 1, nine of ten bootstrap intervals containing
it, while the severity is resolved: under the probe-calibrated protocol at nominal

Fourth, the parameter region in which the theorem applies and the region in
which its exponent is visible in a finite run are different sets: admissible cells fail
the operational criterion and non-admissible ones pass it. These finite-window
results are not tests of, and do not contradict, the cited asymptotic convergence
theorems; every quantity measured is a finite-window fitted slope and not an
asymptotic rate.

1 Introduction
Consensus-based optimization was introduced by Pinnau, Totzeck, Tse and Martin [1]
and given an analytical framework by Carrillo, Choi, Totzeck and Tse [2]. Fornasier,
Klock and Riedl [3] proved convergence in mean-field law under mild assumptions and
combined it with a quantitative mean-field approximation to obtain a convergence
statement for the implementable scheme. Gerber, Hoffmann and Vaes [4] removed the
conditioning on a bounded event and obtained a pathwise mean-field limit at the classical Monte Carlo rate. Related lines include CBO on hypersurfaces [10], anisotropic
variants for high-dimensional machine learning problems [13], a compactness-based
mean-field limit [11], constrained formulations [12] and open implementations [17].
The quantitative theory has moved considerably since. Uniform-in-time meanfield estimates were obtained by Huang and Kouhkouh [6], and by Bayraktar, Ekren
and Zhou [7] in a weak sense on a bounded search domain. Gerber, Hoffmann, Kim
and Vaes [5] proved uniform-in-time propagation of chaos with a prefactor depending
explicitly on the problem parameters and report that for anisotropic noise this prefactor does not depend on the dimension. Koß, Weissmann and Zech [8] treat a class
of consensus methods including CBO and CBS, and Beddrich, Chenchene, Fornasier,
Huang and Wohlmuth [9] study the constrained problem and the practical few-particle
regime.
Our question is therefore not whether the prefactor can be characterized theoretically. It increasingly can. It is which of the parameters governing it are visible in the
fitted exponent of a finite run in the range of ensemble sizes studied here, and how
much of the apparent dimension dependence survives once reference-ensemble initial
concentration is controlled. Behaviour outside the stability condition has been examined before, including in [9], and variants that alter the classical parameter restriction
itself have been proposed, notably consensus-based optimisation with truncated noise
[16]. We claim no priority for studying r > 1; the contribution here is the observability

2

of the classical theory under the tested diffusion regime, not a study of stability thresholds in general. In the literature surveyed for this study we found no prior work that
varies concentration systematically while holding the other experimental factors fixed
and then evaluates the resulting dimension dependence of the fitted finite-ensemble
exponent. The distinction from [9] is worth stating: that work studies how well CBO
optimizes when the particle count is small, which is a question about the quality of the
optimizer, whereas the question here is whether the proven mean-field rate is statistically visible in the fitted exponent of a run of that size, which is a question about what
can be measured. The experiments below examine finite runs in which the benchmark
rate may not be observable; they do not measure optimization quality, and no claim
is made that the configurations studied here optimize well or badly.
There is a concrete version of the gap we address. Figure 1 of [3] is produced on
the Rastrigin objective at α = 1015 , λ = 1, σ = 0.1, ∆t = 0.01 with N = 32 000 agents
in dimension two, and its Figure 2 at α = 1015 , λ = 1, σ = 0.5 with N = 320 000
in dimension one; the parameters are stated in the two captions. The experiment in
[4, Subsection 2.6] verifies the J −1 scaling of the squared coupled error at β = 3,
dimension two, with a reference ensemble of 106 and 100 realizations, and attributes
small-J breakdown to the effective sample size approaching one.

1.1 What is and is not claimed
This paper does not measure the end-to-end guarantee of [3, Theorem 3.8]. The estimator is the coupled distance between a finite ensemble and a large reference ensemble,
which addresses the mean-field approximation component and not the global optimization statement. The discretization step is held fixed at ∆t = 0.01 in both the
finite ensemble and the reference, so that error is common-mode, largely cancels in
the difference, and is not independently identified.
We do not claim any theorem is wrong. That the effective sample size matters at
small J is not new; it is observed in [4, Subsection 2.6].

7 Results
Throughout we write the measured coupled error as EJ ≈ CJ −γ and fit γ̂ by least
squares in log J over the stated window. The theory concerns the pair separately: the
exponent, which is 1 asymptotically in J, and the prefactor C, which carries the dependence on the objective, on β, on the dimension and on the diffusion parameters. This
paper measures γ̂ at finite J and never measures C. A fitted γ̂ below one over a finite
window is therefore not evidence of a different convergence rate: it is consistent with
pre-asymptotic curvature, with reference-ensemble bias, with slope-estimation bias,
with discretization, and with the prefactor being large enough that the asymptotic
regime lies beyond the measured range. Every statement below about γ̂ is a statement
about finite-run observability under the stated protocol and about nothing else.

7.1 The error curve is straight over the measured range
Across 144 cells the slope fitted on J ≤ 160 and on J ≥ 640 differ by mean −0.008 and
median +0.014. A quadratic term in log J was subsequently fitted with a bootstrap
interval; the interval contains zero in 45 of 72 synthetic cells, median curvature −0.014,
and no simultaneous inference is claimed across those cells. A sensitivity grid over the

1280) to −1.025 (lower cutoff 20, upper 5000), a spread of 0.037; at τ0 = 0.1 the tested
fitting-window sensitivity is therefore small. This was not tested at tighter targets and
we do not extend the statement to them. There is no crossover ensemble size within
the measured range at which the proven rate switches on, and a quantity defined as
“the smallest J at which the rate becomes observable” returned the smallest grid point
or off-grid in 137 of 144 cells. The theorem is asymptotic in J and J ≤ 5000 may be
pre-asymptotic; straightness is claimed only for the measured range.

7.5 A sufficient operating level under the tested protocol
The operating-level claim uses twelve synthetic parameter settings from (10), namely
κ ∈ {1, 100} crossed with ρ ∈ {0, 5, 20} and ω ∈ {1, 3}, together with the six named
benchmarks, and we write “eighteen settings” rather than “eighteen landscapes”
because the twelve settings are not twelve distinct functions. When ρ = 0 the cosine
term vanishes and ω has no effect, so (κ, 0, 1) and (κ, 0, 3) are the same function for
each κ; we verified this numerically and the two agree to machine zero. That leaves
ten distinct
Psynthetic functions. Further, (κ, ρ) = (1, 0) gives ai ≡ 1 and reduces
exactly to i x2i , which is the named Sphere benchmark. The eighteen settings therefore represent fifteen distinct objective functions in eighteen parameter configurations,
with one synthetic setting duplicating a named benchmark and two pairs duplicating each other. The duplicated cells are retained as repeated parameter configurations
because the criterion being counted is per configuration, but they are not independent landscapes and the count of eighteen should not be read as eighteen independent
tests. The descriptor study of Section 7.9 uses a different fourteen-landscape panel,
κ ∈ {1, 100} crossed with (ρ, ω) ∈ {(0, 1), (5, 1), (5, 3), (5, 7), (20, 1), (20, 3), (20, 7)},
which adds the ω = 7 ruggedness level needed to give that descriptor a range and
drops the ρ = 0, ω = 3 pair. The two panels share ten landscapes and are not nested;
the count of eighteen refers only to the operating-level panel. Extending to those
twelve synthetic landscapes with the six named benchmarks held out gives 36 of 36
synthetic and 18 of 18 named cells rate-consistent at τ0 = 0.1, and 16 of 36 and 9 of
18 at τ0 = 0.01. The 54 cells at τ0 = 0.1 correspond to 18 parameter configurations
evaluated at three dimensions, not to 18 independent landscapes.
We report τ0 = 0.1 as a sufficient operating level under the tested protocol and
not as a threshold. It is bracketed only between 0.07 and 0.10 by this grid, and it is
not necessary: the level required varies across cells. Recomputed from the archived file

7.6 Reference-ensemble bias
At uncontrolled concentration the d = 2 slope drifted −1.313, −1.205, −1.179 as the
reference grew from 12 500 to 50 000. Under concentration control, fitting γ̂(J∞ ) =
γ∞ + a/J∞ over 12 500 to 100 000 on a common grid capped at J ≤ 1250 gives −0.956,
−1.005, −0.982 for Ackley, Sphere and Rosenbrock against −0.978, −0.985, −1.016 at
J∞ = 50 000; the differences are 0.022, 0.020 and 0.034, and the bootstrap intervals
for the fitted γ∞ overlap those at J∞ = 5 × 104 at every reference size. Against the
dependence-respecting bootstrap slope-SE reference scale of 0.0260 used everywhere
else in this paper, the three differences are of the same order as a single cell’s slope
uncertainty. That is a statement about scale and not a test: the reference scale is
a median standard error and not a confidence bound. The 1/J∞ form is a working
model with no theoretical justification here, and the fit is noise-limited: Rosenbrock’s
J∞ = 25 000 point is −0.872 against −1.010, −1.016, −1.008 at the other three. We
report no evidence of material bias, not a precise limit, and this does not establish
that the bias is negligible; the extrapolated values are used nowhere else.

7.9 A low-dimensional geometry model does not predict the
boundary
Locating the level τcrit at which each landscape ceases to be rate-consistent required
a sweep over eight targets and fourteen synthetic landscapes. Three descriptors were
measured from samples by a procedure identical for synthetic and named objectives
— the condition number of a per-axis quadratic fit, the mean depth of local wells
normalized by that axis’s fitted curvature, and sign changes of the detrended derivative
per unit length — and validated before the sweep against the generating parameters
of (10), giving Spearman correlations +0.928, +0.687 and +1.000 on the subset where
ω is identifiable, worst cross-term 0.491.
For a cell, δ = −1− γ̂ is computed at each of the eight targets. With the benchmark
exponent at −1, a slope flatter than −1 gives a negative δ, and the failure threshold is
δ = −0.15, corresponding to γ̂ = −0.85. τcrit is the crossing of δ = −0.15 obtained by
linear interpolation of δ against log τ between the two adjacent targets that bracket
it. Only cells in which δ is monotone in log τ to within the measured slope-SE scale
are interpolated; cells whose crossing falls outside the grid [0.01, 0.30] are censored,
counted and excluded from the regression, never extrapolated. Worked example: a cell
with γ̂ = −0.88 at τ = 0.05 and γ̂ = −0.82 at τ = 0.03 has δ = −0.12 and −0.18; the

7.10 Inside the parameter condition
Sweeping r from 0.1 to 0.99 with nominal target concentration held fixed, the regression coefficient of γ̂ on r has bootstrap intervals over objectives that exclude an effect
of size 0.10 in all six anisotropic cells and in the two isotropic cells at d = 5, but
not at d = 10, where the coefficients are −0.047 [−0.102, −0.002] at τ0 = 0.1 and
−0.058 [−0.121, −0.015] at τ0 = 0.01. The correct statement is therefore that no monotone dependence of practical size is distinguishable under this design except at d = 10,
where a small negative dependence is detected. An earlier version of this work stated
that r does not move the exponent inside the condition, based on point estimates
compared to a yardstick of the same magnitude; that statement is withdrawn.
The condition is strongly associated with the concentration trajectory. For isotropic
diffusion at d = 40, τ̄ /τ0 falls 3.24 → 2.05 → 0.97 as r rises 0.1 → 0.5 → 0.99, while
anisotropic goes 3.07 → 1.89 → 1.42 and never inverts.
Counts are strongly tolerance-dependent: isotropic 71/93/115 of 144 at tolerances
0.10/0.15/0.20, anisotropic 57/77/86; Bonferroni at 0.15 costs 11 and 9 cells.

7.11 Isotropic against anisotropic
Two matchings are possible and they are not the same comparison. At matched σ the
isotropic system injects d times the diffusion energy; at matched r the two nominal
traces agree exactly at equal state and v, but the matching is nominal and not dynamic.
Paired on the shared realization index in the runs of Section 7.15, and writing the ratio
as anisotropic over isotropic throughout, the realized trace ratio is 1.0003 at r = 0.75
as a ratio of means over 180 pairs, with median 0.9999 and range [0.978, 1.049]; at
r = 3 it is 0.858 with median 0.798 and range [0.554, 4.407]. The independent runs
of Section 7.12 agree: 0.9988 over 576 in-condition pairs and 0.9637 over 288 pairs
at r = 3. Inside the condition the design is energy-matched in realized trajectories
as well as nominally; outside it the realized energies diverge, so the out-of-condition
comparison of Section 7.15 is matched by construction and not by outcome, and any

19

difference found there may be a compound of the diffusion operator and of the state
clouds having separated.
At matched σ and within the condition, the anisotropic-minus-isotropic gap is
+0.004, +0.009, +0.024 at d = 5, 10, 40 for τ0 = 0.1 and −0.005, +0.034, +0.014 for
τ0 = 0.01; every point estimate lies within the prespecified margin of 0.05, the largest
being 0.034, but the point estimates are not the test. Unpaired equivalence testing at
that margin declares 3 of the 18 cells equivalent, leaves 13 inconclusive, and classifies
2 as lying outside the equivalence region. Equivalence is therefore established in three
cells and in no others, and two cells are positively inconsistent with it. We do not
claim that no difference exceeds the margin, because in two cells one does.
At matched energy the picture is different and is reported in Section 7.15 with

7.13 Target concentration is not achieved concentration
The inverse temperature is set by bisection so that the effective sample size fraction
of a probe ensemble of 4 000 points drawn from the initial law equals a prescribed
target. The reference ensemble that is then evolved is a different draw of J∞ points,
so what is held fixed across dimensions is a calibration target and an estimate of β,
not the achieved concentration of the ensemble actually used. Every run that recorded
the achieved fraction lets the discrepancy be measured, and it is not small.
The discrepancy differs by experiment and pooling it overstates the problem where
it matters most, so we report it per experiment. In the concentration arm of Section 7.3,
which produces the headline changes, the ratio of achieved to target has mean 1.0002
and range [0.909, 1.042] at target 0.5, mean 0.962 and range [0.481, 1.141] at 0.1, and
mean 0.810 and range [0.106, 1.363] at 0.01. In the operating-level sweep of Section 7.5
the mean stays between 0.93 and 1.04 at every target while the range widens from
[0.877, 1.188] at 0.3 to [0.111, 4.213] at 0.01. Pooled over the 1 140 cells of all experiments that archived both quantities, the ranges are [0.933, 1.028] at 0.5, [0.357, 1.503]
at 0.1, [0.175, 1.585] at 0.03 and [0.043, 1.850] at 0.01. The calibration is tight where
the weights are diffuse and degrades sharply as the target tightens, spanning a factor of over forty at the tightest target. This matters most exactly where the headline
residual lives: the change of 0.553 reported at τ0 = 0.01 in Section 7.3 is a change at
a nominal target whose realization varies by that much across cells.
The remedy costs nothing in trajectory time: solving for β on the reference ensemble itself rather than on a probe makes the achieved fraction exact by construction. We
applied it. The concentration arm was rerun in full under that change and nothing else
in the design, 144 cells and 2 160 coupled realizations, and the achieved-over-target
ratio is 1.000000 to six decimals in every realization, minimum and maximum alike.
Under this protocol β is solved separately on each realization’s own reference
ensemble, so it is fixed during each trajectory but varies across realizations. The experiment therefore estimates the expectation of γ̂ over the distribution of β induced by
conditioning each replicate on its initial concentration, rather than γ̂ at one fixed
algorithmic β. This is an experimental control device and not the fixed-parameter
algorithm the cited theory studies; we use it because the alternative is to control
nothing.
Two things about this rerun should be stated before its numbers are used. First, it
is a corrective replication: the exact-calibration protocol was adopted after the probe
defect was identified, not specified in advance of the original experiment, and the
criteria applied to it were fixed in the rerun script before that script was first executed.
Second, it is not seed-paired with the probe-calibrated run. The two use different
random streams, so the comparison between them is a replication under a changed
protocol and not a paired intervention on identical draws. The difference between
the two sets of numbers therefore carries the sampling variability of two independent
replications in addition to the effect of the protocol change.
The rerun matters. Only the tight-target point estimate changes by more than
the 0.05 equivalence margin between the two protocols; the moderate-target changes
are 0.024 and 0.040. Because the two protocol runs are not seed-paired, these
point-estimate differences are not interpreted as formal equivalence-test results. The

22

apparent threefold gap between targets 0.5 and 0.1 is not reproduced under exact
calibration. Because the two runs use different random streams and a different calibration protocol, we state that it is not reproduced rather than attributing it to any one
cause. The residual at τ0 = 0.01 falls from 0.553 to 0.253 with interval [0.117, 0.381],
the interval reported in Table 3 under the objective-law sampling unit declared there,
so changing the calibration protocol alone moved the point estimate by 0.300, The
earlier estimate therefore cannot be attributed uniquely to dimension. The two runs
are not seed-paired, so this is the measured effect of a protocol change and not a
decomposition into dimension and calibration parts. What survives is smaller. It is
evidence of a protocol-sensitive concentration-dependent contrast; the analysis on the
non-vacuous window does not resolve a dimension–concentration interaction, and we
do not present the full-grid quantity as evidence of one.
The exact-calibrated values are used for the concentration arm of Section 7.3 and
for every conclusion drawn from it. They are not used anywhere else, because nothing
else was rerun. The probe-calibrated values are retained in Table 3 because the comparison between the two is the measurement of what a plausible-looking calibration
shortcut cost.
Every other experiment in Table 2 remains probe-calibrated: the operating-level
sweep behind the τ0 ≥ 0.1 statement, the regime map, the r sweep and the paired
diffusion comparison. In all of those, τ0 is a target and not an achieved value. This
matters more for the r experiments than it might appear, because Section 7.10 shows
that r itself moves the concentration trajectory: an observed relation from r to γ̂ is
therefore not experimentally orthogonal to concentration, and may run partly through
it. We state their results as holding under the probe-calibrated protocol at nominal

Solving β on the reference ensemble makes ESS0 (J∞ )/J∞ equal to τ0 exactly. It does

of Section 7.3: at τ0 = 0.01 the fifth percentile of [ESS0 (J)/J]/τ0 is 10.000, 5.000 and

below 0.5 the realized concentration of the finite ensemble is systematically further
from target at d = 40 than at d = 2, at every ensemble size measured. Restricting the
slope fit to the non-vacuous window removes the interaction, as Section 7.3 reports,
but it does not remove this.
Controlling the finite-J concentration directly, and what it settles. We ran the one
design that does control it: for each ensemble size a separate βJ is solved on the first
J particles, so that ESS0 (J)/J equals the target exactly at that J, and both the Jensemble and a reference are evolved at that βJ . Over 2 880 reference evolutions the
achieved fraction is 1.000000 to six decimals at every point, against ratios from 0.65
to 19.1 under the nested design. The price is that β now varies along the fitted curve,
so the exponent is a property of a family of algorithms indexed by J rather than of
one algorithm observed at a sequence of sizes. It is a different estimand and we do not
pool it with the rest of the paper.
Under that control the d = 2 to d = 40 change is 0.0156 with interval
[0.0010, 0.0576] at τ0 = 0.5 and 0.0964 with [0.0059, 0.2265] at τ0 = 0.01, against the
nested values of 0.054 and 0.078 on the same window. The pre-specified decision rule
had three branches: the tight-target change falling inside the moderate-target interval would have withdrawn the residual, remaining above it would have established
that the residual survives, and overlap without resolution would mean the experiment
was not powered. The third branch obtains: the interval was too wide to discriminate the hypotheses under the stated design. The interval at the tight target is 0.221
wide, which is wider than the effect being tested. Repeating the bootstrap on the
finer (objective, realization) unit rather than the pre-specified objective unit gives
[0.0069, 0.2087], essentially the same width, so the coarse unit is not the reason.
The honest conclusion is therefore narrow. Finite-J concentration can be controlled, and controlling it does not make the dimension-associated contrast disappear,
but this experiment cannot establish whether the contrast that remains is dimension
or is something else. The residual of 0.078 in Section 7.3 stays confounded, and we
report it as confounded rather than as an effect of dimension.

9 Limitations
The reference ensemble is a proxy at one twentieth of the size in [4]; Section 7.6 finds
no evidence of material bias under concentration control, but the extrapolation is
noise-limited and assumes a 1/J∞ form without justification. The horizon is varied
only over {0.5, 1, 2}. The concentration-control and regime-map experiments use the
centred law; Section 7.7 varies it explicitly across four laws. Concentration is controlled
at t = 0 except in Section 7.4. The modality descriptor of Section 7.9 retains a residual
correlation of +0.58 with frequency across the full synthetic family. Section 4 is upper
bounds composed with upper bounds. Theorem 4 covers one of three error terms and
its Chebyshev step is crude. No familywise error guarantee is claimed; Bonferroni
sensitivity is reported and the out-of-condition anisotropic count in Section 7.15 falls
from 18 to 8 of 75 under it. The comparison with [5] is between a finite-run fitted
exponent and a bound prefactor, which are different objects. Section 7.15 does not
identify the mechanism of the out-of-condition degradation.

10 Conclusions
The r experiments remain target-calibrated rather than exact-calibrated, so their
effect estimates may contain residual concentration confounding; only the concentration arm was rerun. The boundary r = 1 separates the theorem-admissible from the
non-admissible parameter regime, but it does not coincide with a finite-run observability threshold: admissible cells can fail the operational criterion, as three of the
thirty in Section 7.12 do, and non-admissible ones can pass it. That the two regions
are different objects is the paper’s central conceptual point.
One distinction governs how everything below should be read. This paper measures
whether the J −1 benchmark exponent is visible in the fitted slope of a finite run.
It does not measure optimization performance, and a flat fitted exponent is not a
statement that CBO optimized badly in that configuration. A method can optimize
well in a regime in which no proven rate is observable, and nothing here licenses the
reverse inference.
Finite-ensemble observability of the CBO mean-field rate is not determined by
dimension alone. Much of the apparent dimension-dependent degradation seen under
an inverse-temperature rule matched by objective spread is mediated by Boltzmannweight concentration: with the inverse temperature solved on the reference ensemble
itself, so that the achieved initial concentration equals its target exactly, controlling
concentration reduces the change in fitted exponent from d = 2 to d = 40 from 0.96 to

What remains after both corrections is small, and an audit of the nested subensembles
shows that even that remainder is confounded with a dimension-dependent finite-J

here.

and does not determine the interacting-particle behaviour; the observed behaviour is
consistent with the scaling.

exponent on both axes in three of four panels, and leaves their interaction unresolved:
the pre-specified rule required three of four interval exclusions with a consistent sign
and one was obtained. Beyond the parameter condition, the onset location cannot
be distinguished from r = 1 on any grid we ran, and that experiment is not powered to locate it; the severity is the result that is resolved, growing at +0.144 per
doubling of dimension for isotropic diffusion on the non-admissible contrast, interval

diffusion over the tested dimensions, −0.002 with interval [−0.030, +0.027]. Paired on
shared realizations the two types change sign between r = 1.5 and r = 2 at d = 40
and d = 80, with the standing caveat that the energy matching holds nominally and
not in realized trajectories once r ≥ 2.
Some published CBO demonstrations use very large fixed inverse temperatures —
α = 1015 in the two numerical illustrations of [3], the Rastrigin experiment in dimension two with N = 32 000 agents, λ = 1, σ = 0.1, ∆t = 0.01, and the one-dimensional
variance comparison with N = 320 000 and σ = 0.5 — at which the weights can
become numerically saturated, in which case the consensus point is effectively the arg
min. Corollary 7 shows the underlying weight concentration is not an artefact of the
floating-point format. It is also the regime in which we can measure no proven rate at
all.
